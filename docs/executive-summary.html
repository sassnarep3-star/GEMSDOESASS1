Q1 · Why did 0.2778 win?

Because 6,436 dots were deleted, not because a better fault was found.

Reproduced from bytes this session, not from anyone's notes.

The reproduction

champion = 0.2600 file − (dots within 200 m of a known fault)

The published GEMSDOE25 raster dotted-h19-5-d2-8 has 44,090 dots and scored 0.2600. Deleting the 6,436 dots that sit within 2 px (200 m) of the mapped catalogue leaves 37,654 dots and scored 0.2778. We recomputed that operation here and got the GEMSDOE32 file with a symmetric difference of 0 — an exact byte-level reproduction. The champion is a strict subset of the 0.2600 file.

Verified locally: c32 ⊂ d2-8 (37,654 of 44,090); d2-8 minus (catalogue ≤ 2 px) = 37,654, symmetric difference 0.

The mechanism

False positives cost 0.2 each; misses cost 0.8 each

The metric credits each hidden-fault pixel by the best prediction within 300 m, so extra dots near the same fault add nothing, while every dot far from any fault adds α = 0.2 to the denominator. Dots sitting on a fault that is already in the public catalogue therefore earn zero credit and cost full false-positive weight. Deleting them is pure gain: 0.2600 → 0.2778.

Certified identity: D = α·(TP + FP) + β·K, exact because α + β = 1. Oracle ceiling = 1.000. See metric_certification.json.

The uncomfortable corollary. Under this metric, one unit of extra true coverage is worth about 14 units of false-positive mass at the champion's operating point (37.7 in the certification fixture). Recall dominates — but only where the field is right. Thinning dots uniformly is not the fix; finding better lineaments is.

Next hypotheses

Five untried ideas, ranked by expected gain against cost.

Each names the layers, the physical signature, why it should catch an unmapped fault, and what could mimic it.

Rank 1 · highest expected gain

Pluvial-shoreline rejection on the lineament support

Layers: det_elev (12), det_elev_slope (19) plus an absolute-elevation surface. Signature: along-strike elevation variance. A Lake-Lahontan shoreline is a topographic lineament at near-constant elevation; a range-front fault is not. Why it gains: shorelines are almost certainly a large share of the false lineaments that hold precision g down near 0.065, and removing them raises g directly — the only lever the metric rewards. Mimic: a fault that reactivates an old shoreline scarp. Cost: low, but it needs an absolute DEM (currently outside the outbound allowlist).

Rank 2

Basement-step continuation across the basin floor

Layers: depth_to_base_surf (15), iso_grav_anom (13), iso_grav_anom_hg (18). Signature: a coherent linear step in the basement/gravity surface that continues beyond where the topographic scarp dies out. Why it gains: buried or blind faults have no scarp at all, so every topography-based field — including the champion — is blind to them by construction, while the potential-field step survives. Mimic: a depositional basin margin or a paleo-channel edge. Cost: medium; the bands are already restored locally.

Rank 3

Geodetic strain-rate lineaments, uncorrelated with topography

Layers: geod_2ndinv (4), geod_shearrate (7), geod_dilaterate (8). Signature: ridges in the strain-rate second invariant that do not coincide with a scarp. Why it gains: it is the only channel in the stack measuring present-day deformation rather than accumulated offset, so it is the least likely to have been used by the mappers who built the public catalogue. Mimic: poroelastic or hydrological transient strain. Cost: low.

Rank 4

Radiometric alteration haloes along structure

Layers: GeoDAWN radiometrics K, Th, U, TC and the ratios ThK, UK, UTh (restored locally). Signature: K-enrichment / U-Th depletion haloes elongated along a lineament. Why it gains: hydrothermal alteration is the actual exploration target, and it marks fluid-flowing faults — a sub-population no purely structural field selects for. Mimic: a lithologic contact between tuff and basalt. Cost: medium.

Rank 5 · cheapest validation win

Stop trusting the catalogue holdout

The finding: the champion's field has exactly zero pixels in common with the public catalogue (minimum distance 1.0 px) — it was built to exclude mapped faults. Our holdout's truth is the catalogue. So the instrument systematically penalises the field that wins live and rewards fields that predict already-mapped faults. Why it gains: until a truth set of genuinely unmapped faults exists, every “holdout improvement” may be an improvement towards the wrong target. Cost: zero — it changes how the next four ideas are judged.
